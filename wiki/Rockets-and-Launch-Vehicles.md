# Rockets and Launch Vehicles

[← Back to Wiki Home](README.md)

## How Rockets Work

Rockets use **Newton's third law** — for every action there is an equal and
opposite reaction. By expelling propellant mass at high speed out of a nozzle,
a rocket generates thrust in the opposite direction. Unlike jet engines,
rockets carry their own oxidizer, allowing them to operate in the vacuum of
space.

## Key Concepts

- **Stages** — Most rockets are built in stages, each with its own engines and
  fuel. Stages are jettisoned once their fuel is spent, reducing the mass the
  remaining engines must lift.
- **Delta-v** — The change in velocity a vehicle can achieve; a key metric for
  mission planning.
- **Reusability** — Modern vehicles like SpaceX's Falcon 9 and Starship are
  designed to land and fly again, dramatically reducing launch costs compared
  to traditional expendable rockets.

## Notable Launch Vehicles

| Vehicle | Operator | Era | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Saturn V** | NASA | 1967–1973 | Carried Apollo missions to the Moon; most powerful rocket flown to date at the time |
| **Space Shuttle** | NASA | 1981–2011 | Partially reusable crewed spacecraft |
| **Soyuz** | Roscosmos | 1966–present | Longest-serving crewed launch vehicle family |
| **Falcon 9** | SpaceX | 2010–present | First orbital-class reusable rocket |
| **Falcon Heavy** | SpaceX | 2018–present | Currently one of the most powerful operational rockets |
| **Ariane 5/6** | ESA | 1996–present | European heavy-lift launcher |
| **Starship** | SpaceX | In development | Designed for full reusability and Mars missions |
| **SLS** (Space Launch System) | NASA | 2022–present | Powers the Artemis lunar program |
